# Program Design Lessons

Evidence-backed lessons for shaping programs, drawn from the Explore: Godot cohort (retro + session debriefs), the UTP GameLab 4.0 debrief, the Jordan 2025 debrief, and the cross-partner feedback log.

## 1. Hands-on first -- theory as reflection, not primer

- Modding-first lowers the barrier to early wins. Session 1 of Explore: Godot started modding with only ~35 minutes left; the revised target is hands-on within the first 30 minutes.
- Move the game design framework to *after* the modding block, as a reflection lens. Partner-led observation (Urban Arts) showed the same pattern: concepts get compressed anyway, so design for it.
- Mini challenges ("make it harder", "make it work for one player") work as creative on-ramps.
- Glasswing observation: engagement is highest in artistic and analog activities (paper level design), lower in technical Godot sessions -- sequencing should ride that gradient.

## 2. Pacing is systematically underestimated

- 60-minute deck pacing did not match real 90-minute session needs; sessions consistently ran tight. Setup and installs eat real time (Backstitch alone cost ~10 minutes).
- UTP learners rated workload outside sessions a blocker (21.2%) and personal obligations were the single biggest blocker (48.2%). Attendance drop of ~24-25% mid-program is consistent across GameLab 2.0, 3.0, and 4.0 -- and it correlates with the Git introduction and peak academic periods.
- Ánima: students found timing overwhelming and lacked basic digital skills -- scope must match the digital baseline of the population, not the design team's assumptions.
- Recommendation from UTP debrief: extend sessions to 90+ minutes OR reduce scope for the time available. Don't do neither.

## 3. Git is the #1 cross-program blocker

Consistent across UTP, Jordan, VRmont, Urban Arts, and Glasswing. Command line, branching, and PRs blocked nearly all first-time contributors; "GitHub took time away from creativity." Responses in flight or recommended: short Spanish-language Git tutorials, a Godot on-ramp before StoryQuest work, ZIP upload as a non-Git delivery path (in progress), and platform-native submission (gap). Any program whose completion requires a Git workflow will lose most learners at that step.

## 4. Scope contributions small

- Only 3 StoryQuests were submitted from ~234 GameLab 4.0 learners. Full StoryQuests (intro + outro + multiple mini games) are too ambitious for first-time contributors; the validated target is 1 mini game.
- Ánima teams mostly failed to complete the full scope; no StoryQuest PR was submitted.
- Smaller, incremental contribution units with starter templates and pacing tools are the repeated recommendation.

## 5. The teaching-to-self-directed transition must be scaffolded

UTP's most-cited pedagogy complaint: the first sessions were "classes", then abruptly "go do it now." Teams ghosted; cameras-off culture made collaboration harder. Explore: Godot handled this better with a deliberate mid-program transition session (planning anchors: level identity, biggest unknown first, problem decomposition) -- that structure is reusable.

## 6. Mentorship is the engine -- and the bottleneck

- Mentors are the #1 motivator (92.9% at UTP). Certificates are #2 (91.8%).
- One expert mentor doesn't scale: individualized help for unique learner projects is the hardest thing to systematise. At 50+, add TAs at ~1:40 (consistent with other Act Two programs); use a triage breakout model (lobby → quick triage → deep-dive room); consider Zoom over Google Meet for self-join breakouts.
- Capture what mentors improvise: supplemental, technically rooted guides are "necessary, not optional" (identified by Session 4). A Godot/Backstitch FAQ bot trained on session transcripts is proposed.
- Mentor onboarding needs to include maintainer skills, StoryQuest creation, and licensing -- at UTP, very little actual PR review occurred because mentors lacked these.

## 7. Facilitator quality decides partner-led outcomes

- Facilitators who use the speaker notes and interaction prompts succeed; the Glasswing instructor who lectured through slides complained the program was "too long, too many slides, not enough hands-on" -- in front of students.
- Training works (Urban Arts CPO: "fantastic... team is already buzzing") but needs more Q&A time (BGC).
- Curriculum is a strength: praised by BGC, works across ages, instructors can join one session and pick up. Protect that property.
- Local adaptations (Glasswing translating and simplifying materials) are valuable but need traceability between designed and delivered program.

## 8. Language and localization are structural, not cosmetic

English materials slowed Jordan classes (instructors translated live); UTP learners asked for Spanish tutorials; language silos formed in shared Discord channels; Glasswing adapted English materials. Spanish is a first-class language of the community. Materials, tutorials, and facilitator resources should be planned multilingual from the start.

## 9. Shared vocabulary with partners

Glasswing's "session" (a classroom meeting) diverged from Endless's "session" (1 hour of content), producing sites reporting "session 6" while on Endless Session 1. Proposal: "encuentro" for the physical meeting, "sesión" for the content unit, plus a per-site protocol for how many content units per meeting. Generalize this: define nomenclature in every partner agreement.

## 10. Cohort composition shapes what you learn

The first Explore: Godot cohort skewed experienced -- great energy, strong showcase, useful mod library, but it may mask how the program performs with true beginners. Jordan saw the same spread problem in reverse (university learners found it too easy; wide gaps required differentiated pacing). Distinct beginner/advanced tracks are a recurring recommendation.

## 11. Deliverables must land on the platform

Glasswing site visits found strong student work saved locally, "locked in each classroom" -- no quantifiable record of progress. Platform submission has to be integrated into session flow, not treated as optional homework. This is also what makes impact reporting possible.

## Decision levers

- Session length standard (90+ min) vs scope reduction -- pick one explicitly per program.
- Whether microcredential eligibility can tolerate non-Git submission paths (the answer determines completion rates in schools).
- How much of the mentor function to productize (guides, FAQ bot, triage model) vs staff (TAs) at each scale tier.
- Beginner track investment vs qualifying the "no experience required" marketing promise.
- A standing localization budget line per program rather than post-hoc translation.

# Community Needs

What the always-on community requires -- philosophy, personas, channel strategy, and the concrete platform gaps blocking it.

## Philosophy: the garden

The team frames community as a garden: the platform provides soil (infrastructure), climate (safety and culture), and trellis (programs and structure); learners grow at their own pace. A learner finishing a program is a **harvest, not churn** -- two successful outcomes: **Perennials** (stay and contribute as moderators, maintainers, mentors) and **Seeds** (leave with a portable portfolio; may return as Pollinators -- industry mentors, guest speakers).

### Personas

- **Seedling** (new learner) -- fragile; needs quick wins and low barriers; high-friction contribution paths cause drop-off
- **Wildflower** (self-guided) -- thrives on open content, jams, and community without structured cohorts
- **Companion Plant** (contributor/mentor) -- enriches the space for others; burnout risk must be tracked ("returning active contributors" is a key metric)
- **Pollinator** (educator/partner) -- cross-pollinates between the broad community and partner cohorts

### Operating principles

Friendly, not friends (mentors, not peers -- no private access or favoritism). Public first (questions answered where everyone can see). FAIL forward (celebrate visible failure; badges/XP can recognize sharing one). Democratize attention (spread sunlight evenly). Reduce tech friction (platform-native contribution paths before GitHub PRs). Automate community rituals (the space must feel alive without staff). No learner leaves without a next step. Scale through ecosystem management, not individual care.

## The hard warning from the field

Ceibal's Sergio Pérez, after a platform demo: teenagers categorize platforms like this as "homework tools" -- once that mental model sets in, they disengage from everything else. And bluntly: **"If we only have the platform, there will be no engagement."** The feed shown in the demo had very limited student interaction. This is a positioning and experience problem, not just a feature checklist -- the question is how to earn teenagers' attention.

Corroborating evidence: peer feedback between Explore: Godot sessions was near-zero; feedback notifications failed silently, so learners never saw any of the instructor's written feedback or playtests; at UTP, learners abandoned shared channels for private Discord servers, language silos formed, and help-seeking defaulted to DMing staff.

## The 13 community capability gaps (vs Circle, shared May 28, 2026)

From running Explore: Godot on Circle alongside the platform: Spaces & space groups (fit-for-purpose areas, gated unlocks); DMs & group chats (welcome messages, quiet-member outreach); Introductions space; Location & interest groups; Shoutouts space (durable recognition); Sharing space (where retention kicks in); Gamification that is visible (points buried in a profile do nothing); Richer member profiles; Member directory; Workflows & automations ("what works at 20 members breaks at 200"); Data & reporting (currently flying blind on at-risk members); Pinned & featured content; Events & calendar (session logistics currently live entirely off-platform).

Related running observations from sessions: editable comments, threaded replies, rich link previews, embedded playable links in posts, cohort filtering of task progress, undoable task approvals, richer media in feedback, and tools to find and organize peer work (a single chronological feed becomes unmanageable with multiple cohorts).

## Channel strategy

**Platform** is where learners live. **Discord** is the conversation home for the *Threadbare* contributor community -- educators, facilitators, mentors, 18+ learners, and the games team. It sits at the Contributor → Career stage, not the beginning. It is not the platform, not a program delivery tool, not a metrics goal, and not a space for minors. Server principles: roles drive channel visibility; cohort channels are temporary (archived at program end so learners graduate into the broader community); Spanish is a first-class language; architecture extensible to Arabic and others. Planned: embed #threadbare-development as a read-only feed on the platform game page.

**Loom Lounge** (weekly YouTube live stream) demystifies game making and showcases contributors -- aimed at the Contributor → Career transition, measured by platform engagement it drives, not viewership.

## Contribution pathways: the community's growth engine

The gaming-community scenario in the service blueprint shows the pattern: discovery, play, and Discord participation all work today, but the first contribution hits a wall -- fan art and music currently require a GitHub PR. Missing: non-Git asset submission, structured playtest forms with recognition, community challenges/jams with tangible rewards, XP/badges for contributions outside programs, automated celebration rituals, and any pathway from community contributor to program learner ("learn to make games like this"). ZIP upload is in progress as a stopgap.

Success is measured at the platform level: contributor activation rate, contributor retention over 90-day windows, response time in key channels, and task completion coordinated through the community.

## What partners ask of community features

Urban Arts wishlist: closed groups (paired classrooms across geographies), guidance on how to give feedback (by domain, criteria, examples), "mod as feedback," recommendation systems, open source/IP clarity for remixed games, sharing achievements to external career tools (LinkedIn). Glasswing: co-branded certificates. Ceibal: cohort-level analytics and review filtering (also an educator-community need).

## Decision levers

- Positioning first: what makes the platform feel like a creative community rather than a homework tool is the upstream decision; features follow from it.
- Which of the 13 capability gaps to build natively vs bridge with external tools -- automations, events, and notifications are the three that field evidence says break programs today (notifications already did).
- Recognition visibility: gamification only works if status is visible where people interact, which is a design stance, not a feature toggle.
- Non-Git contribution paths determine whether Seedlings and the gaming community can ever contribute -- this is the single biggest unlock for the Contributor stage.
- How much community liveliness to automate (rituals, milestones, prompts) before scale makes manual care impossible.

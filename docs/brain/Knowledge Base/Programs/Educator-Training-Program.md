# Educator Training Program (English)

Extracted 4 August 2026 from the English source decks in Google Drive. The facilitator training deck plus the facilitator playbook.

**Program shape:** this is the training track offered to educators who join, the content a teacher works through in order to run *Core: Threadbare* with students. There is no formal certification, exam, or badge. Stated participation requirements are: pre-survey, the hero sprite activity, joining the Endless Educators program with access code `EndlessEducators` and uploading a screenshot plus .png of the hero with design rationale, post-survey, and joining Discord.

### Core: Threadbare Overview

This guide will prepare you to lead learners on an adventure in the whimsical, worn world of *Threadbare* - a journey of restoration where stories weave the fabric of a forgotten world back together.

Long ago, the First Storyweaver spun the Sacred Elements - Memory, Imagination, and Spirit - into threads and wove them on the Eternal Loom, creating the Weave, the living fabric of reality. But now the world is unraveling. The Eternal Loom lies dormant at Fray's End, and the last Elders cling to fading tales. Dark forces - Ink Drinkers, Memory Miners, and Spirit Stealers - roam the lands, devouring the essence of culture, identity, and language.

Your learners will become Storyweavers, called to reclaim lost stories and restore the Weave. Through StoryQuests - interactive journeys of their own making - learners will design characters, shape meaningful choices, and build imaginative encounters that invite others into the world of *Threadbare*.

### Pedagogy

We follow a simple yet effective approach: **play**, **create**, **share**, and **reflect**. In other words, game makers will explore existing games (play), make their own games (create), provide feedback on one another's games (share), and refine their ideas (reflect), fostering collaboration and mutual learning.

This method taps into the power of experiential learning, allowing game makers to learn by doing and to engage directly with the material. It also aligns with constructivist principles, emphasizing hands-on experiences that build knowledge and understanding.

By playing, creating, sharing, and reflecting, game makers not only develop essential game design skills but also enhance their creativity, critical thinking, and collaboration abilities.

Throughout these sessions, game makers will engage in activities that are labeled with play, create, share, and reflect icons, as shown below. Providing a balance of these essential pedagogical practices across the learning program supports engagement and achievement of learner outcomes.

### Future-ready Skills

In today's rapidly changing world, future-ready skills are indispensable, offering learners the versatility and adaptability needed to thrive across diverse industries, including but not limited to gaming. From critical thinking, systems integration, and problem-solving to adaptability and ethical decision-making, these competencies are essential for success in various fields. By cultivating these skills, aspiring game makers in our programs not only position themselves to excel in dynamic environments but also contribute positively to shaping a more innovative, inclusive, and resilient future across multiple sectors.

#### Critical Thinking and Problem Solving

By applying core concepts from gameplay mechanics, player experience, and aesthetics, alongside their practical application of the Godot Game Engine, learners will strengthen their ability to analyze and break down complex design challenges - a skill that extends well beyond game design to all areas of learning.

#### Creativity and Innovation

Crafting an original game experience encourages game makers to brainstorm unique game ideas, develop and test prototypes, and iterate on their work.

#### Digital Literacy

By using relevant tools to create prototypes and other game design artifacts, game makers will gain proficiency in technology used in the game development industry.

#### Project Management

Through planning, organizing, and executing new game experiences, game makers acquire skills essential for effectively completing tasks and meeting deadlines.

#### Collaboration and Communication

Engaging with other game makers to create, playtest, and provide feedback on each other's games cultivates teamwork and the ability to convey ideas clearly, preparing game makers for collaborative environments in the game development industry.

#### Inclusivity and Diversity Awareness

Focusing on inclusivity and diversity in game design fosters awareness and empathy, equipping game makers with the skills to create games that represent and appeal to diverse audiences.

#### Presentation Skills

Through delivering game pitches and engaging in communication activities, game makers refine their ability to clearly present and adapt their ideas to different audiences in professional settings.

#### Adaptability and Flexibility

By navigating the iterative process of game design and responding to feedback, game makers develop the ability to adjust and succeed when faced with novel and unscripted challenges, an important trait for success in dynamic and evolving industries like game development.

#### Ethical Decision Making

Considering ethical implications in game design encourages game makers to make responsible decisions about content and representation, and challenges them to apply this awareness directly to their designs.

#### Resilience and Perseverance

Embracing challenges and setbacks inherent in game design, game makers shift their perspectives on "failure", a crucial quality for overcoming obstacles and achieving success in creative endeavors.

### Technical Skills

In today's tech-driven world, mastering technical skills is essential for aspiring game makers and beyond. From grasping basic coding concepts to navigating game development tools and creating visual art and animations, these skills are fundamental for crafting engaging games. Understanding concepts like sound design, UI creation, and game testing techniques enhances game quality, while learning collaboration tools and communication tech fosters effective teamwork. Exploring different platforms and game distribution methods broadens horizons and prepares learners for diverse opportunities beyond gaming, reflecting the universal relevance of these technical skills in our increasingly digital landscape.

#### Basic Coding Skills

By applying simple game logic and interactions using user-friendly tools, game makers will gain a foundational understanding of coding concepts.

#### Familiarity with Game Development Tools

By creating and modifying game elements, game makers will become comfortable using beginner-friendly game design software without needing extensive coding knowledge.

#### Exploration of Platform Options

Through exploration of various features and elements that are essential to game engines, game makers will have a better understanding of the capabilities and limitations of different creative platforms.

#### Basic Game Testing Techniques

By identifying and fixing common issues in their games, and focusing on ensuring basic functionality and playability, game makers will learn and develop their game testing techniques.

#### Introduction to Game Distribution

Learners will gain an understanding of how games are distributed to players, exploring basic methods for sharing their games online or with friends and family.

### Essential Questions

Each session begins with an essential question designed to provoke deep thinking about key concepts in game design. For instance, asking "What makes a level 'just right'?" encourages game makers to delve into critical aspects such as game mechanics, storytelling, player experience, and player engagement. These thought-provoking questions serve as a foundation for exploration and learning throughout the session.

As a facilitator, you should regularly refer back to the essential question during the session, guiding game makers to make connections between their learning activities and the broader question. This ongoing reflection helps students think critically about what contributes to a successful game. It also fosters the development of crucial skills in creativity, teamwork, and problem-solving.

By consistently engaging with the essential question, game makers not only deepen their understanding of game design principles but also enhance their ability to articulate and apply these concepts in their projects. The goal is for game makers to be able to thoughtfully construct their own answer to the essential question by the end of each session, demonstrating their learning and growth in the field of game design.

### Milestones

In Core: Threadbare, each StoryQuest is broken into five milestones, which structure the project into clear, manageable stages. Milestones help learners track progress, plan work, and focus on key game design goals, while providing natural checkpoints for mentors and facilitators to give guidance and feedback.

#### Milestone 1: Ideation

- **Objective:** Explore *Threadbare* and brainstorm StoryQuest ideas.
- **Key Tasks:** Play *Threadbare*, reflect on the purpose of a StoryQuest, form a team, brainstorm StoryQuest ideas, and create an initial StoryQuest Overview in the Game Design Document (GDD).
- **Outcome:** Learners generate ideas for characters, narrative structure, mini-games, and initial GDD entries.

#### Milestone 2: Intro & Outro

- **Objective:** Set up the development environment and begin creating the StoryQuest's introduction and conclusion.
- **Key Tasks:** Set up GitHub, install Godot, clone the repository, import *Threadbare*, duplicate and rename template files, design tilemaps, characters, art assets, and narrative text for intro/outro scenes, and connect scenes.
- **Outcome:** Learners complete playable intro and outro scenes that reflect their StoryQuest's aesthetics and narrative.

#### Milestone 3: Mini-Game(s)

- **Objective:** Design and implement up to three mini-games within the StoryQuest.
- **Key Tasks:** Design and replace tilemaps, characters, and art assets for each mini-game, modify mechanics using available mini-game components, add narrative text for each mini-game, connect each mini-game scene to the next StoryQuest scene, and, optionally, add music or sound effects to enhance gameplay.
- **Outcome:** Learners create functional, polished mini-games integrated into the StoryQuest, providing varied gameplay experiences and reinforcing narrative and mechanics across multiple challenges.

#### Milestone 4: Playtesting & Iteration

- **Objective:** Test, gather feedback, and refine the StoryQuest.
- **Key Tasks:** Conduct team, peer, and mentor playtesting; analyze feedback; prioritize and implement changes; playtest refinements.
- **Outcome:** Learners improve gameplay, narrative flow, and user experience through iterative design.

#### Milestone 5: Production & Launch

- **Objective:** Finalize and share the completed StoryQuest.
- **Key Tasks:** Create a pitch deck, present the StoryQuest to an audience, publish the StoryQuest with title, description, and contributor information.
- **Outcome:** Learners deliver a complete, playable StoryQuest, clearly communicated and shared publicly.

### Assessment

Assessment in Core: Threadbare is task- and skill-based, focused on learners completing StoryQuest tasks with clearly defined acceptance criteria. Each task is pre-labeled with the relevant domain, subdomain, and future-ready skill. Learners self-assign tasks, complete them, and submit for review, and mentors or project maintainers verify completion. Accepted tasks award XP, reflecting the effort, difficulty, and skills applied.

Facilitators support learners by observing collaboration, problem-solving, and reflection, helping learners connect each task to broader skills and domains. Resources and tutorials in GitHub Discussions provide guidance and reference material, enabling learners to explore independently. The assessment system emphasizes hands-on progress, meaningful skill development, and growth through real-world game-making experiences, rather than traditional grades.

#### Domains and Subdomains

##### Game Design

**Core Gameplay & Mechanics** - Build the fundamental systems that make the game fun to play. *Examples:* Create player movement and abilities, design puzzle mechanics.

**Game Feel & UX** - Make the game feel responsive and satisfying to interact with. *Examples:* Add screen shake when hit, polish movement animations, build visual and audio player feedback systems.

**Narrative & Storytelling** - Create story content and dialogue that engages players. *Examples:* Write character conversations, create cut-scenes, design environmental storytelling elements.

**Progression & Motivation** - Design systems that keep players coming back for more. *Examples:* Create point scoring, build character leveling systems, offer achievement rewards.

##### Art

**Animation & Effects** - Add movement and visual effects to game elements. *Examples:* Animate characters, add particle effects.

**Aesthetics & Visual Design** - Create and implement visual elements that define how the game looks and feels. *Examples:* Design character sprites, create tilesets, choose color palettes.

**Music & Sound** - Implement audio that enhances gameplay. *Examples:* Add background music, create sound effects.

##### Engineering

**Gameplay & Systems Programming** - Implement game functionality through code and other game engine tools. *Examples:* Create scripts, configure node properties, create custom resources.

**Optimization & Performance** - Ensure games run efficiently and provide a smooth player experience. *Examples:* Debug code errors, reduce lag and asset file size, improve loading times.

##### Go-to-Market

**Marketing** - Promote games and connect with players to build awareness and interest. *Examples:* Create social media campaigns, analyze target audiences, develop promotional materials.

**Publishing & Distribution** - Get games to players through the right platforms and channels. *Examples:* Submit games to app stores, manage release schedules, optimize for different platforms.

##### Management & Production

**Quality Assurance & Playtesting** - Test games to find problems and ensure they work as intended. *Examples:* Find and report bugs, test game difficulty, organize playtesting sessions and gather player feedback.

**Project & Workflow Management** - Organize tasks and coordinate team efforts to complete projects on time. *Examples:* Create task lists, set deadlines, track project progress.

#### Future-Ready Skills (assessment labels)

##### Problem Solving

Identifying challenges in game design and finding effective solutions through iteration, testing, and adaptation. *Examples:* Debugging mechanics that don't work as intended; balancing difficulty or reward systems; resolving conflicts between mechanics and narrative.

##### Creativity & Design Thinking

Generating and refining original ideas, designing innovative game elements, and approaching challenges with curiosity and experimentation. *Examples:* Designing new characters, levels, or story content; iterating on gameplay mechanics or visual design; prototyping multiple solutions for a game problem.

##### Technical Literacy

Using game development tools effectively to implement, prototype, and polish mechanics, aesthetics, and functionality. *Examples:* Writing scripts to implement gameplay systems; animating characters, configuring UI, or adding audio; optimizing assets and mechanics for smooth performance.

### Session 1: "Why do we play games? Why do we mod them?"

> Note: The document's Essential Questions table lists the Session 1 essential question as "What makes a video game a game?" while the Session 1 heading reads "Why do we play games? Why do we mod them?" Both appear in the source.

#### Overview

This opening session acknowledges the significant step game makers take as they learn to use Godot, a full professional game engine. It's important to provide them with the opportunity to familiarize themselves with the basics: opening Godot, loading a game, playing it, and making simple modifications. This introduction is designed to encourage learners to actively engage with the engine and uncover its possibilities on their own and to reflect on the new possibilities that a game engine like Godot offers them as creators.

Once in Godot, the session focuses on modifying games, or "modding," with an emphasis on encouraging learners to explore the user interface (UI) of Godot as they seek creative ways to improve the provided game. The moddable Pong game is ready to play as soon as it's loaded into Godot, and it includes a variety of additional assets - images, scenes, sounds, and songs - within the project. These resources allow learners to mod the game without needing to search for or create new assets outside the engine, enabling them to concentrate on mastering the basic tools and functions within Godot.

#### Preparation

To prepare for this opening session, install the latest version of the Godot game engine onto each computer that your learners will be using. It is recommended that you keep both this written session plan and the [Core: Godot | Sessions](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1f16F6MIOOBs_NADKE7CKcCsHvuQWubtjQfFt6qdoNBo/edit?usp=sharing) slides open on your instructor device. Any links to content will be placed here and in the presenter notes of the accompanying slides.

Prior to the session, please watch the following introductory videos for using Godot: [For Instructors: Installing A Game In Godot](https://youtu.be/9Jl8eq8cyzM?feature=shared) and [For Instructors: Godot Interface Basics](https://youtu.be/wvB-ijin0mE?feature=shared). Learners will discover Godot through their efforts to mod the pre-made game. However, your ability to give hints or clues within the Godot UI will encourage more exploration and help learners get a sense for the many options they have to modify the game.

Please set up any online tools needed, such as breakout rooms, online whiteboards, and digital sticky notes. For in-person settings, prepare materials like posters, sticky notes, and pens. Test all technical equipment in advance to ensure Godot is running correctly and that you have smooth video playback and interaction on the session slides.

#### Time

Approximately 60 minutes

#### Extensions

If you have additional time, the following extensions should be considered: Encourage participants to explore a different game element to modify as there are a significant number of ways in which they can alter the game without any programming experience. Additionally, to extend the learning experience, encourage participants to add their own imagery or audio to their modded game.

#### Assessment

During the session instructors can informally assess game makers' progress in their familiarization of Godot by asking questions about what they have learned about different panels within the engine. Game makers will likely not know the engine's terminology for the scene tree (top left) or the inspector (on the right) or the file system (bottom left), but they may be able to describe what these panels can offer them in their attempts to mod the game. Terminology can come with time but the idea here is to informally gather evidence of a general understanding of the core panels in Godot.

For game design and modding, during the sharing out of their mods, assess game makers on their reasoning for the changes they made and how they might enhance the player experience, as well as their ability to correctly identify which elements of a game these mods may have impacted.

### Session 2: "Should game makers also be game players?"

#### Overview

In this session, game makers will start by playing a selection of platformer games. They will start a game journal to capture their thoughts on what they liked, didn't like, and how they felt while playing. This reflective practice, inspired by Tracy Fullerton's [*Game Design Workshop. A playcentric approach to creating innovative games*](https://www.gamedesignworkshop.com/), is crucial for two reasons. First, it helps game makers think critically about how minor changes and differences can influence the player experience, and second, they are being asked to record how certain choices in design make them feel as a player.

Following this, game makers will load a moddable platformer into Godot and, drawing from their journal insights, make mods that enhance the feel of the gameplay. The emphasis is on the consideration of how their changes will impact the player experience. The session wraps up with game makers sharing their mods and reflecting on the tools and resources they used, fostering a deeper appreciation of thoughtful game design and additional practice with the Godot engine.

#### Preparation

For this session, ensure that you have the link to the [moddable platformer](https://github.com/endlessm/moddable-platformer/) game ready to share with your game makers. However, please confirm that Godot is still functioning correctly on all devices.

As the instructor, it might be handy to keep this session guide and the Sessions slides open on your device to help guide the session effectively. Your game makers will need to have both Godot and any learner resources open during this session. This will allow them to follow along with the instructions and actively participate in the modding process.

#### Extensions

#### Assessment

### Session 3: "How can game makers use constraints to their advantage?"

#### Overview

The focus in this session will be on exploring problem decomposition, a fundamental computer science principle. Game makers will learn to break down the complex task of level design into smaller, manageable parts. This will allow them to approach the design process in a way that splits the design into simpler tasks providing learners more manageable first steps.

Game makers will then begin the transition from making small mods and tweaks to an existing platformer to creating their own complete level in the next two sessions. Working in small teams of two or three, they will practice how to collaborate effectively to design and build a new level that enhances the player experience within the constraints of the base demo game.

#### Preparation

In this session, while there is some light computer use (videos, optional digital brainstorming tools), Godot will not be needed. It is recommended that you have the session slides open to help guide students as usual, and that students have access to any video or resource links as needed.

#### Time

Approximately 60 minutes

#### Extensions

If you have additional time, the following extensions should be considered: Encourage participants to apply the process of decomposition even further within their level design breakdown by assigning roles and responsibilities to each of their tasks. This will better prepare them to get started on their levels in the following session as they will not only have made themselves some simpler tasks to work on but they will also know who is doing what.

#### Assessment

Instructors should assess if learners are breaking down tasks into smaller steps during their level design brainstorming. After practicing this in the survival activity, students should now apply the same skill to their platformer level. Look for clear main tasks and logical subtasks in their planning.

### Session 4: "What makes a level 'just right'?"

#### Overview

In this hands-on working session, small teams of learners will design a new level for a 2D platformer using the Godot game engine. It is important to remember that for first-time game makers this process will likely be messy. Learning is not linear! They will now put their ideas into practice and experiment with tools and mechanics to bring their ideas to life.

Learners will focus on:

- How to creatively expand on the mechanics and aesthetics of the demo game without fundamentally changing the gameplay.
- How to create balance between challenge and accessibility for the player.
- How to design the level so that it progressively becomes more difficult as the player moves through it, introducing new challenges, mechanics, or obstacles that increase in complexity. This will push them to consider pacing and how difficulty curves can enhance the player experience.

The instructor will guide game makers as they design their levels. Remind them to:

- Use their level design decomposition from the previous session to break the large task of designing the level into smaller, more manageable pieces. This will help them to get started and be able to focus on one task at a time.
- Refer to the components in the moddable platformer that they are extending if they are looking for ideas on what to build or add.
- Playtest their level sections. Adjustments based on playtesting will help ensure the level is fun, challenging, and fair.

### Session 6: "What's the difference between a game idea and a game that gets published?"

#### Overview

In this session, game makers will begin preparing for their first collaborative game by brainstorming creative ideas and forming teams. This session focuses on generating and sharing ideas to help define the direction of their projects and creating balanced teams with complementary interests and skills. Learners will brainstorm game concepts, work together to refine their ideas, and begin laying the groundwork for their Game Design Documents (GDDs). By the end of the session, teams will have a clear starting point and a framework for planning their games.

The key ideas introduced in this session are the following:

- **Create a formal plan:** Designers should come up with a formal plan for their games and try to think through as much of the scope of their project as possible.
- **Reflect and Refine:** Designers also need to be flexible and allow room for new creative ideas they have along the way and, just as importantly, incorporate feedback from players.

Game makers will begin formalizing their brainstorming on a [Game Design Document](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_design_document) to help them organize and track their ideas for what they will make. An abbreviated version of this document (Game Design Document or GDD) will ask learners to describe key elements of their design idea: the title of their level, basic concept, core mechanics, and overall goals/challenges.

#### Preparation

For this session it will be important that each game maker has access to and can make a copy of the [GDD document](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1-Dvyg65dilVw3j1_y1oN_-Pfv1QBGb5xJJP7m_ns_bM/edit?usp=sharing) for their work going forward.

#### Time

Approximately 60 minutes

#### Extensions

It is advisable here to treat the GDD as its own potential extension. The more detailed and thoughtful game makers are with this document the better off they will be working as a team on their final levels. Rather than adding an additional component to this session, you can encourage game makers to review their GDDs and look for elements of their plan that may benefit from some problem decomposition or further breakdown of any tasks/goals. This will ideally provide some smaller more concrete starting points for their working sessions.

#### Assessment

Instructors should look for evidence of some of the following within the session:

##### Thoroughness of Game Design Document (GDD):

- The GDD contains clear, well-thought-out descriptions for key elements such as the game title, concept, core mechanics, and goals/challenges.
- Students have detailed their ideas in the GDD, demonstrating deep thinking about their game design, with attention to both big-picture ideas and smaller components.
- Evidence that the team has collaborated to fill out the GDD comprehensively, with each member contributing to various sections.

##### Formal Planning and Scope Management:

- The team has articulated a structured, realistic plan for their game level that considers the full scope of their project, including a timeline or development steps.
- They show an awareness of potential challenges and have proposed ways to address these in their planning document.
- The team has made an effort to assign some roles and responsibilities to help divide the work.
- Their GDD reflects an openness to adapt and improve, with a section dedicated to reflection on how they plan to iterate on their initial ideas.

### Session 18: "Where do we go from here?"

#### Overview

In this session game makers will focus on showcasing their game projects and reflecting on their achievements. The session begins with game makers reflecting on their experience, followed by presentations of their games in a showcase format. This celebratory event provides an opportunity for recognition and feedback, fostering a sense of accomplishment among the game makers. The session concludes with a closing question to promote excitement about future opportunities in game design.

#### Preparation

Preparation for this session involves coordinating the logistics of the game showcase, whether it's virtual or physical. Instructors should ensure that game makers have access to the necessary platforms or spaces to present their games effectively. Additionally, considerations should be made for any awards or badges to be distributed, and arrangements for collecting feedback from participants should be put in place.

#### Time

Approximately 60 minutes.

#### Extensions

Extension ideas for this session could include inviting industry professionals or peers from other classes to attend the showcase as guest reviewers, providing an opportunity for game makers to receive diverse perspectives and feedback. Additionally, instructors could organize a post-showcase reflection session where game makers discuss their experiences and lessons learned throughout the game design process.

#### Assessment

The formal assessment component, which can be measured with the [Core: Rubric for Learners](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1Rb9EvE-ULdwNnljLTKXzWaWRlf5xyHZYW4xsEPJpZJc/edit) and/or [Core: Rubric for Instructors](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1aLooit7aArEYvTs4NMXZHZDDP0SciQ3pyJRjevYEp9w/edit?usp=sharing), will focus on evaluating the following deliverable requirements:

##### Create a Playable Game

- Did we apply game-making concepts? Did we use important game design ideas like the elements of a game, core gameplay loops, and player experiences?
- Did we collaborate to ideate and iterate? Did we work well as a team to brainstorm ideas and make improvements based on feedback?
- Did we consider multiple perspectives? Did we think about different viewpoints and feedback from others when making our game?

##### Communicate Our Game Idea

- Did we explain the components of our game? Did we clearly describe all the important parts of our game, like the story, characters, and rules?
- Did we persuade with a strong presentation? Did we make our game idea exciting and convincing to our audience?
- Did we reflect on performance and next steps? Did we think about what went well, what could be better, and what we should do next?

### Appendix - Glossary

#### A

**Aesthetics** - The visual and artistic aspects of a game, including colors, character design, and environmental elements that contribute to the player's experience.

**Art** - A domain of game making that involves the visual and audio components of a game, including character design, environmental assets, sound design, UI elements, and animations.

**Assessment** - Methods used to evaluate learners' progress, including informal reflection and formal rubrics.

**Asset (Game Asset)** - Any visual, audio, or interactive element used in a game, such as characters, backgrounds, music, sound effects, or animations.

#### B

**Branch** - A version of a Git project where changes can be made without affecting the main branch. Branches allow game makers to experiment or add features safely.

#### C

**Cloning** - The process of creating a local copy of a repository from a remote server, such as GitHub.

**Collaboration** - The process of working together to design, create, and refine game projects, often involving feedback and playtesting.

**Commit** - A save point in Git that records the changes you selected. Commits create a history of your project that you can go back to if needed.

**Core Gameplay Loop** - The repeated cycle of player actions, rewards, and challenges that keep the game engaging.

**Creative Agency** - The ability of learners to take ownership of their projects and make independent creative decisions.

#### D

**Decomposition (Problem Decomposition)** - The process of breaking down complex game design tasks into smaller, manageable parts.

**Deliverables** - Tangible outcomes expected from learners, such as a playable game and a game pitch presentation.

**Diversity & Inclusivity** - Ensuring that games represent and are accessible to players from a variety of backgrounds and experiences.

**Domain** - A specific area of expertise within game development that focuses on a distinct aspect of the creation process (Engineering, Marketing, Game Design, Art, and Management and Production).

#### E

**Engagement** - The level of involvement and interaction learners have with the game-making process.

**Engineering** - A domain of game making that involves the technical aspects of game development, including programming, physics simulation, and system architecture using the Godot engine.

**Experiential Learning** - Learning through hands-on activities, exploration, and iteration rather than passive instruction.

#### F

**Feedback** - Constructive critique provided by peers, instructors, or mentors to help refine game designs.

**Feature Branch** - A branch created to work on a specific feature or change in a project. It can later be merged back into the main branch after review.

**Fork** - A copy of a repository created under your own GitHub account, allowing you to make changes freely without affecting the original project.

**Future-Ready Skills** - Essential competencies such as problem-solving, collaboration, and adaptability that prepare learners for careers in and beyond game development.

#### G

**Game** - An interactive experience designed to engage players through challenges, choices, and feedback, often providing entertainment, education, or storytelling.

**Game Design** - A domain of game making that involves the creative process of designing gameplay mechanics, player interactions, level structures, and overall game flow.

**Game Design Document (GDD)** - A structured outline that details the concept, mechanics, story, and visuals of a game.

**Game Engine** - A software framework used to develop video games, providing essential tools for rendering graphics, handling physics, scripting gameplay mechanics, and managing assets.

**Game Maker** - A learner or creator engaged in designing, developing, and refining video games, applying principles of game design, mechanics, storytelling, and user experience.

**Git** - A version control system used to track changes in code, manage different project versions, and collaborate with others (eg, GitHub).

**Git Bash (Terminal/Command Line)** - A text-based interface where Git commands are typed to interact with a repository, such as creating branches or committing changes.

**Git Command** - An instruction typed into the terminal to tell Git what to do, such as git status to see the state of your project.

**Go-To-Market** - A domain of game making that involves the strategies for launching a game, including audience research, marketing, distribution, and community engagement.

**Godot Engine** - A free and open-source game engine used for designing and developing games in the Core: Godot program.

#### I

**Iteration** - The process of refining a game through repeated cycles of prototyping, playtesting, and feedback.

#### L

**Learner Outcomes** - The knowledge and skills learners are expected to gain by completing the program, such as understanding game mechanics and creating a playable game.

**Level** - A distinct section of a game, designed with specific challenges, objectives, and environments.

**Level Design** - The process of designing and structuring the spaces within a game, ensuring progression, challenges, and engagement.

**Local Repository** - The version of a Git project stored on your own computer, where you make and save changes before sharing them online.

#### M

**Management & Production** - A domain of game making that involves the process of organizing and leading a game development project, including teamwork, workflow management, and iterative development.

**Main Branch** - The central branch of a Git project that usually represents the official version. Finished and reviewed changes from feature branches are merged here.

**Mechanics** - The rules and systems that define how a game functions and how players interact with it.

**Mentors** - Industry professionals who provide guidance, insights, and feedback to learners throughout the game-making process.

**Merge** - The process of integrating changes from one branch into another in an open-source project.

**Merge Conflict** - When branches are merged, Git attempts to combine their changes into a single branch. Conflicts may arise if the changes affect the same parts of a file, requiring manual resolution before the merge can be completed.

**Modding** - Modifying an existing game to experiment with game mechanics, aesthetics, and player experiences.

#### O

**Open Source** - Software with a publicly accessible source code that can be freely used, modified, and shared by anyone, often developed collaboratively by a community of contributors.

#### P

**Pedagogy (Play, Create, Share, Reflect)** - The learning approach used in the Core: Godot program, emphasizing hands-on creation, collaboration, and iterative improvement.

**Player** - An individual who interacts with a game, engaging with its mechanics, challenges, and narrative to achieve objectives or explore its world.

**Playtesting** - The process of testing a game with players to gather feedback and improve gameplay.

**Prototyping** - Creating an early version of a game to explore mechanics and test ideas before finalizing the design.

**Pull (Git)** - The action of updating your local repository by bringing in the latest commits from a remote repository (like GitHub). A pull moves changes from GitHub into your computer.

**Pull Request (PR)** - A request made on GitHub to bring changes from your branch or fork into another branch, usually the main project. A pull request is not a direct command, but a step for review and approval before changes are merged.

#### R

**Remote Repository** - The version of a Git project stored online (for example, on GitHub) that allows you to back up your work and collaborate with others.

**Repository** - A project folder tracked by Git that contains all of the files, history, and branches for a game or program.

#### S

**Scaffolding** - Providing structured support to help learners develop skills progressively before working independently.

**Session Structure** - The organization of each learning session, including essential questions, activities, and assessments.

**Side Quests** - Supplementary learning activities that allow learners to explore specific topics in game design at their own pace.

**Staging** - The step in Git where you pick and choose which files or changes to prepare for the next commit, like selecting which edits in your game you want to save.

**Systems Thinking** - Understanding how different elements in a game interact and influence each other.

#### T

**Technical Skills** - Skills related to coding, using game engines, designing UI, and implementing sound and animations in a game.

**TileMap** - A grid-based system in Godot used for designing and organizing game environments.

#### U

**Untracked Files** - Files that exist in your game project and "working folder", but are not yet being tracked by Git. Git only saves them once you add and commit them.

**User Experience (UX)** - The overall feel and accessibility of a game from the player's perspective.

**User Interface (UI)** - The visual elements of a game, such as menus, buttons, and HUDs, that allow players to interact with the game.

#### V

**Version Control System (VCS)** - A tool that tracks changes to files and projects over time, letting multiple people collaborate and return to earlier versions if needed.

## Milestone 4 -- Playtesting & Iteration

### Session 4.1 (Session 14) -- What makes a StoryQuest good?

#### Title slide

**Session 14**

**What makes a StoryQuest good?**

Agenda listed on the title slide:

- Have you shared your StoryQuest with others?
- Share your prototype: to infinity and beyond
- Plan a Playtesting Session
- Create a web page for your prototype
- Peer Playtest

> Facilitator note: **Overview**
>
> This is the first session of a new Milestone: #4, Playtesting and Iteration. This session and the ones that follow focus on sharing prototypes with "the outside world," so [playtesting sessions](https://github.com/StoryQuest-Project-Board/threadbare/issues/23) will be held to collect information, in order to later on polish the StoryQuests. The first step in sharing the prototypes will be making playable links for each fork available, so on the [Endless Studios platform](https://studio.endlessstudios.com/studio/), each team can create a web page for their project. This way, they'll be able to share their work with classmates. The second step is to take the prototype even further -- sharing it with friends, family members, and others, using more targeted feedback forms.
>
> "Outsider" input provides a fresh perspective: because they are not involved in the games' creation, playtesters can easily identify bugs and inconsistencies in addition to other potential improvements. For example, if team makes a StoryQuest about climate change, questions for playtesters might include: Does the game convey awareness about climate change? In what way? Are you more interested in learning about climate change after playing? Game makers can refine their work based on the answers they receive during playtesting.

> Facilitator note: **Preparation**
>
> In this session, game makers will follow the Project Board issue [Conduct playtesting sessions](https://github.com/StoryQuest-Project-Board/threadbare/issues/23) (there they will also find the tutorial [Enable playable links for a Threadbare fork](https://github.com/endlessm/threadbare/discussions/1123), which was already proposed in Milestone 3 and is essential for these playtesting stages).
>
> This is a good moment to reflect: Is there a defined target audience? Can the developers reach out to classmates, friends, family members, or other people who fit into the chosen audience category? And what if there isn't a defined target audience and anyone could play and enjoy the StoryQuest? That scenario might actually be an even better opportunity to gather data on how the prototype works. Taking note of first impressions, bugs, and other feedback will help improve both the overall goal of the StoryQuest and each of its mini-games.
>
> As always, please set up any online tools needed, such as breakout rooms, online whiteboards, and digital sticky notes. For in-person settings, prepare materials like posters, sticky notes, and pens. Test all technical equipment in advance to ensure everything is running correctly and that you have smooth video playback and interaction on the session slides.

**Have you shared your StoryQuest with others?**

> Facilitator note:
> - Welcome game makers and ask them the opening question: "Have you shared your StoryQuest with others?"
> - Encourage game makers to answer the question even if the answer is "no". Ask follow up questions with an open mind: for ex., if they were insecure about sharing their prototype with someone because they knew there was still a lot to polish, share with them the value of playtests at any stage of game development. A fresh perspective catch things that the developers cannot (because they are too close to the work), and it also allows game makers to test the target audience (if it was taken into consideration when planning).
>
> For online environments: Encourage game makers to turn on their cameras. They can use the raise hand feature or respond in chat.
>
> Time Saving Tip [for in-person settings]: To avoid a long, whole-group discussion, participants can share their answers with a peer near them.

#### Activity: Plan a playtesting session -- 15 min

**Plan a playtesting session**

> Facilitator note:
> - Share with the participants the issue they'll be working on during this session: [Conduct playtesting sessions](https://github.com/StoryQuest-Project-Board/threadbare/issues/23).
> - Show the video [Playtesting Considerations](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=90w4GbTiQlE) included in the issue's tutorial.
> - Then ask game makers what kind of information they'd like to get from playtesters. Have them reflect with their team and take notes (on paper or in a shared document):
> - Is their StoryQuest suitable for all ages in terms of skills and content? Or was it designed with a specific player profile in mind? Who is the potential target audience?
> - What other things from the shared video could be useful to them?
> - By defining these parameters, game makers will be able to make improvements to their StoryQuest later on.

**Peer playtest**

> - Remind learners that to fill out the Feedback Form for someone else's StoryQuest, they need to click the "Leave feedback" button, and that the form is already preloaded on the page.
> - Keep in mind that the form is very short and quick to complete, so you can structure this activity in a way that lets game makers playtest more than one prototype. That would be ideal!
> - Emphasize that each player should complete the form after finishing their play session(s).
> - Let teams know that they can find the results of the completed forms in the feed on their own StoryQuest web page.
> - Finally, invite all participants to play each other's prototypes.

> During the last session, Session 18, all teams will showcase the product of their hard work by giving a presentation that will take the form of a Pitch. This is a common practice in the video game industry that opens the game to the whole world: it's a tool to find investors, clients, players, etc. So during Session 17, game makers with get familiar with what a pitch is, and will put together the final presentation.

> Facilitator note: **Preparation**

The more examples they get, the better! A good strategy is also to encourage learners to investigate on their own, and to share their findings with team members, and also the rest of their peers. This will be proposed during this session, but as an optional way to learn about pitches (recommended, if time allows it or if learners struggle with the new concept).
>
> Take into account how many teams are in your group, and provide in advance (during this session) a time frame for each presentation. In this way, learners will know whether they will have 2 min, 5 min or more to showcase their StoryQuest (and will be able to plan accordingly). Read through Sessions 17 and 18 to make sure you are guiding them fairly.

> Facilitator note:

> - Wait for their responses and help refine the definition until it gets close to the one below.
> - Pitches are a powerful tool for persuading potential investors, clients, and players. There's no single standard for what a video game pitch must include, but there are some common key points that are usually communicated:
> - Story, genre, and core mechanics of the game
> - Unique selling points
> - Target demographic
> - Concept art or images from the prototype
> - If time allows, choose one of the Resources listed below to show.
> - Since game makers are working within the already existing game *Threadbare*, they'll be "selling" their StoryQuest as a way to get people to play it.
>
> For online environments: Encourage game makers to turn on their cameras. They can use the raise hand feature or respond in chat.

> Time Saving Tip [for in-person settings]: To avoid a long, whole-group discussion, participants can share their answers with a peer near them.

#### Activity: What's unique about your StoryQuest? -- 25 min
